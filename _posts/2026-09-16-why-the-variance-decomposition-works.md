The law of total variance is usually introduced as an identity to be verified:

$$\Var{Y} = \E{\Var{Y \mid X}} + \Var{\E{Y \mid X}}.$$

You expand both sides, the cross terms cancel, and the result sits there looking arbitrary. It isn't. It is the Pythagorean theorem, and the cancellation is an orthogonality statement in disguise.

## The setup

Work in $L^2(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \Prob{\cdot})$: random variables with finite second moment, with inner product

$$\langle U, V \rangle = \E{UV}, \qquad \|U\|^2 = \E{U^2}.$$

This is a Hilbert space, and that is the whole content of the argument. Let $\mathcal{G} = \sigma(X)$ be the sub-$\sigma$-algebra generated by $X$, and let $\mathcal{H} \subset L^2$ be the closed subspace of $\mathcal{G}$-measurable square-integrable variables.

<div class="definition" markdown="1">
For $Y \in L^2$, the conditional expectation $\E{Y \mid X}$ is the orthogonal projection of $Y$ onto $\mathcal{H}$.
</div>

This is a *characterisation*, not a computational recipe, and it is the one that makes the identity obvious.

## The right angle

Write $\hat{Y} = \E{Y \mid X}$ and decompose

$$Y = \hat{Y} + (Y - \hat{Y}).$$

By the defining property of an orthogonal projection, the residual $Y - \hat Y$ is orthogonal to every element of $\mathcal{H}$ — in particular to $\hat Y$ itself.

<div class="theorem" markdown="1">
If $\E{Y} = 0$, then $\Var{Y} = \|\hat{Y}\|^2 + \|Y - \hat{Y}\|^2$.
</div>

<div class="proof" markdown="1">
Since $\E{Y} = 0$ we have $\Var{Y} = \|Y\|^2$. Expanding,

$$\|Y\|^2 = \|\hat{Y} + (Y-\hat{Y})\|^2 = \|\hat{Y}\|^2 + 2\langle \hat{Y}, Y - \hat{Y}\rangle + \|Y-\hat{Y}\|^2,$$

and the middle term vanishes because $\hat Y \in \mathcal{H}$ while $Y - \hat Y \perp \mathcal{H}$.
</div>

Now read the two surviving terms. Because $\E{\hat Y} = \E{Y} = 0$ by the tower property, $\|\hat Y\|^2 = \Var{\E{Y \mid X}}$. And by the tower property again,

$$\|Y - \hat{Y}\|^2 = \E{(Y - \hat Y)^2} = \E{\E{(Y-\hat Y)^2 \mid X}} = \E{\Var{Y \mid X}}.$$

That is the identity. The centring assumption $\E{Y}=0$ costs nothing, since both sides are unchanged by replacing $Y$ with $Y - \E{Y}$.

## What this buys you

Three things fall out immediately that are awkward to see from the algebra:

1. **The inequality $\Var{\E{Y\mid X}} \le \Var{Y}$** is just "a leg is no longer than the hypotenuse." Conditioning on $X$ cannot manufacture variance.
2. **Equality holds iff $Y$ is $\sigma(X)$-measurable** — the residual leg has length zero exactly when $Y$ already lies in the subspace.
3. **$\E{Y \mid X}$ minimises $\E{(Y - g(X))^2}$ over all measurable $g$.** This is not a separate theorem about regression; it is the statement that the projection is the nearest point in the subspace, which is what projections do.

Point 3 is worth dwelling on. "Regression is conditional expectation" is stated in every course, and derived by calculus in most of them. Geometrically there is nothing to derive.

---

**A caveat on where the geometry stops.** All of this needs $\E{Y^2} < \infty$. Conditional expectation exists more generally, for $Y \in L^1$, but there it is defined by the partial-averaging property rather than by projection — $L^1$ is not an inner-product space, so there is no right angle to appeal to. The Hilbert-space picture is a genuine special case, not the general definition.
